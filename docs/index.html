the problem

One tier per task is the same mistake, one level down.

An agent working a ticket takes many steps, and they are not equally hard. Reading a failing test, tracing a bug across six modules, and updating a changelog line are not the same job — but a fixed model choice charges all three at the price of the worst one. Picking one tier per task instead of per call just moves the waste rather than removing it.

Fixed on the big model

Nothing ever fails for being under-powered. You pay top rate for changelog edits, and nothing in your logs ever tells you that you did.

Fixed on the small model

Cheap until the step that needs more, then silently wrong output, a retry you noticed late, or a human cleaning up. The failure is the expensive part.

Per-step routing

Each step priced on what it involves, with a way back up when the cheap tier turns out to be wrong — and a record of every time that happened.

how a decision is made

Category is a weak signal. Context is not.

“Write a unit test” is fifteen lines or a six-hour slog through mocked dependencies. What predicts difficulty is everything around the label, so category only breaks ties — it never drives the decision.

Precedence

  1. An engineer's tier hint is trusted outright and skips inference. People usually know when a task is hairy, and that signal is cheaper and more accurate than anything inferred.
  2. The heuristic scorer, when its confidence clears the threshold. No network call, sub-millisecond.
  3. An LLM classifier, for the ambiguous middle only — scores sitting near a cut, where the heuristic genuinely cannot tell.

A min_tier floor is applied last, so a task can be pinned above whatever the classifiers concluded.

The raw sum is normalised to 0–1 and cut at 0.30 and 0.70. Every constant here is a starting value, not a law — the loop below moves them.

Why confidence is continuous

Confidence is the distance from the nearest cut. A score sitting on a cut has confidence 0 — exactly the task worth spending a cheap classifier call on. Everything else resolves for free.

It matters that this is a smooth number rather than a handful of buckets. If confidence could only take four values, moving a threshold slightly would either change nothing or change everything, and a tuner that can only overshoot is worse than none. The continuity is what makes the outer loop possible at all.

the two loops

It routes per step, and it learns from what happened.

Inner loop — the tier climbs for one hard step, then drops back

Each step is routed on its own. A step that comes back wrong escalates without restarting the task, and the next step starts fresh rather than inheriting the higher tier. A real run of the bundled example:

step 0lowread the failing test
step 1hightrace across modules
step 2mediumtests fail →
step 2 retryhightests pass
step 3mediumpackage-wide rename
step 4lowchangelog entry

One escalation, three de-escalations.

Outer loop — each cut moves on the failures of the tier it governs

Outcomes you report go to a decision log. The tuner reads it, joins each decision to what happened, and moves the thresholds those decisions came from — per boundary, never on one aggregate rate: 

$ tierwise tune routing.jsonl --rework-cost 0.05
cost per successful step: $0.0167
  low_medium   n=12  fail=0.00  expl=6/4   target=0.22 [cost-derived]  0.30 → 0.33  relaxed
  medium_high  n=6   fail=0.67  expl=6/0   target=0.50 [cost-derived]  0.70 → 0.67  tightened

Two independent conclusions from one log, moving in opposite directions. All the failures landing in low says nothing about the medium/high cut, and moving both would push medium-tier work to the top tier on the strength of failures that happened elsewhere.

That run used the same illustrative per-step prices as the table above, so cost_per_success there is a shape, not a rate.

Evidence is spent once

A watermark records which outcomes have been acted on. Re-running over the same log changes nothing and says so, which is what makes the tuner safe on a cron — otherwise one bad week would be re-counted every night until routing hit the floor.

Bounded on purpose

Twenty outcomes per boundary before it moves, one 0.03 step per run, a hard floor and ceiling, an enforced gap between cuts, and a logged event for every change.

Errors are not tier failures

A timeout or a rate limit is an infrastructure problem. It is recorded, it does not escalate, and it never counts as evidence — a bigger model cannot fix a connection.

Failure is asymmetric, and that is the hard part

Under-provisioning shows up in outcome data. Over-provisioning never does: the top tier does not fail work a cheap tier could have handled, so a step that succeeded at medium looks identical whether or not low would have done. No amount of ordinary logging finds it.

So a fraction of steps can be deliberately routed one tier below the recommendation. expl=6/4 above means six downgrades tried, four succeeded — the only direct evidence that the cheap side has room. A downgrade that fails escalates straight back up, which is what makes exploring affordable. It is off by default, and never crosses an explicit hint or floor. 

from tierwise import Router, RouterConfig

Router(config=RouterConfig(exploration_rate=0.05))   # 5% of eligible steps

What rate should it steer to?

The default target failure rate is a hand-picked 0.10. Tell it what a failure actually costs you and each boundary derives its own instead — trying cheap first always costs the cheap run, plus, when it fails, the expensive run anyway and whatever the failure itself cost: 

p* = (c_expensive − c_cheap) / (c_expensive + rework)

Tier costs are read from the log. Rework is the one term the log cannot supply, so without it nothing is guessed and the fixed target applies. cost_per_success is reported every run — tier is a proxy, that is the number the loop is really moving.

Two explicit overrides

tier_hint is trusted outright and skips inference. min_tier sets a floor without pinning the tier. Exploration is forbidden from crossing either.

Turning the drift off

Auto-applied tuning is the one genuinely unsupervised part. Pass explicit thresholds and the tuned file is ignored entirely; or tune with --dry-run and commit the changes yourself.

from tierwise import Router, Thresholds

Router(thresholds=Thresholds())   # pinned; never reads the tuned file
portability

Any provider. Nothing here knows who serves the models.

Scoring, sessions, escalation, telemetry, thresholds and the tuner contain no provider code at all. Three small contracts are the whole integration surface, and the Anthropic helpers are optional conveniences behind an extra — the base install has no runtime dependencies.

1. Model names

A plain Tier → str mapping. Nothing is parsed or validated; the string comes back as decision.model.

2. The classifier transport

A str → str callable. Prompt in, JSON out. Any provider, a gateway, a local model, or a cached lookup.

3. The executor

A function of the decision. The runner never inspects the result — your verify and cost_fn interpret it.

from tierwise import LLMClassifier, ModelMap, Router, TaskRunner, Tier

router = Router(
    model_map=ModelMap({Tier.LOW: "gpt-5-mini",
                        Tier.MEDIUM: "llama-4-70b",
                        Tier.HIGH: "gemini-3-pro"}),
    classifier=LLMClassifier(call_fn=my_gateway_call),
)
runner = TaskRunner(executor=my_executor, verify=lambda r: tests_pass(), router=router)

Model names belong in a tierwise.toml you commit, so CI, the tuning cron and every developer resolve the same ones. tierwise models shows where each came from, and marks anything unset as a built-in rather than a choice: 

$ tierwise models
low     gpt-5-mini                   [config]
medium  llama-4-70b                  [config]
high    claude-opus-5              [env]
get started

Three lines, then your existing call.

# not on PyPI yet
pip install -e /path/to/tierwise
pip install "tierwise @ git+https://github.com/Kibrom1/tierwise.git"
from tierwise import Router, TaskSignals

router = Router()
decision = router.route(TaskSignals(category="bugfix", file_count=4, lines_changed=150))

client.messages.create(model=decision.model, ...)  # unchanged

Two shortcuts that skip signal estimation entirely: tier_hint=Tier.HIGH when you already know, and min_tier=Tier.MEDIUM to set a floor without pinning.

Letting it drive

TaskRunner does the plumbing — route, call, check, escalate, retry at the new tier, record, stop at the cap — while the two judgements stay yours. 

from tierwise import TaskRunner, make_anthropic_executor

runner = TaskRunner(
    executor=make_anthropic_executor(),        # you own the call
    verify=lambda response: tests_pass(),      # you own the standard
    cost_fn=lambda response: price(response),
)

result = runner.run(TaskSignals(...))
result.ok, result.tier, result.tiers, result.total_cost_usd

The loop is exactly as good as verify. “The response was non-empty” gives you a loop that escalates on noise and then teaches the tuner from it — point it at your tests.

Don't skip to stage 4. The tuner is only as good as your definition of failure — without a signal you trust, it trains on noise.

questions

Things worth knowing before you wire it in.

Does it call the model for me?

Not by itself. route() returns a decision whose .model is a string; your code makes the call. That is why any provider works — owning the call would mean owning auth, retries, streaming, tool use and rate limits.

TaskRunner will run the whole loop for you, but it calls an executor you supply, so the same boundary holds.

Does it work with providers other than Anthropic?

Yes. Only three of eleven modules mention a provider at all, and the SDK is imported lazily in two optional factory functions. The full loop — routing, escalation, telemetry, tuning — runs with invented model names on a machine with no provider SDK installed.

The built-in default names are Anthropic IDs, so set your own first; they are labelled default so a built-in never reads as a choice you made.

What if I have no reliable way to say whether a step succeeded?

Then use stages 1–2 and stop there. Routing works without outcomes; escalation and all learning do not. A weak verifier is worse than none once the loop is automatic — it escalates on noise and then teaches the tuner from it.

Will it change my routing behind my back?

Only when you run the tuner, and only within the bounds described above. A watermark stops the same evidence being counted twice, so a cron cannot compound one bad week.

To remove the drift entirely, pass explicit Thresholds — the tuned file is then ignored — or tune with --dry-run and commit the changes yourself.

Where do the signals come from?

You supply them. file_count and lines_changed come free from git diff --numstat — a helper ships in the example. requires_context, ambiguity and dependency_depth are judgement calls your orchestrator makes.

When you already know the answer, say so: a tier_hint is trusted outright and skips inference entirely.

How often should I run the tuner?

On a schedule against accumulated logs — nightly, or per sprint — not after every task. It needs twenty outcomes per boundary before that boundary moves, and re-running over an unchanged log does nothing.

Does exploration make my agent worse?

Slightly and deliberately, in exchange for the only evidence that over-provisioning exists. A downgrade that fails escalates straight back up, so the cost of being wrong is one extra call. It is off by default; 0.05 is a reasonable place to start.

It never explores against a tier_hint or below a min_tier floor — those are people saying what a task needs.

Is it on PyPI?

Not yet. Install from a local checkout or straight from the Git URL. Python 3.10+, no runtime dependencies; anthropic and pytest are extras.

before you rely on it

What it does not do.

Each of these is answered at length in the FAQ.

01

It does not extract the signals. Wrong signals in, wrong tier out.

02

It is only as good as your verifier. A weak check teaches the tuner from noise.

03

The built-in model names are defaults, and they age. Set your own before the first call.

04

Three tiers is the model. Five meaningful sizes, or routing across providers by price, would be collapsing something finer.

05

Auto-applied tuning drifts on its own. Bounded — and still drift.
